\section{Receiver Energy}
The energy consumption of the receiver can be estimated from the number of real multiplications \(N_M\) and real additions \(N_A\) it performs to process each symbol. The true cost of these operations depends on the hardware implementation. Here the reference values shown in \cref{tab:energy} are used to allow for comparison between different DSP algorithms. An accurate estimate of their actual energy consumption would require analysis of their ASIC implementation for a modern process node.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Energy cost of arithmetic operations for different bit widths \cite{horowitz2014computing}.}
    \label{tab:energy}
    \begin{tabular}{ccc}
        \toprule
        \(n\) (bits) & Add (pJ) & Multiply (pJ) \\
        \midrule
        8  & 0.03 & 0.2 \\
        32 & 0.1  & 3.1 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Dependence on Word Length}
A straightforward adder implementation requires \(n\) bit-wise additions when operating on words of length \(n\). Hence fitting a linear relationship through the origin to \cref{tab:energy} gives an estimate for the energy of an \(n\)-bit addition \(E_A(n) = 3.16n \mathrm{\ fJ}\). Multiplication requires the accumulation of \(n\) partial products of length \(n\), so scales with \(n^2\). Fitting a quadratic through the origin to \cref{tab:energy} gives the estimated energy of an \(n\)-bit multiplication \(E_M(n) = 3.03n^2 \mathrm{\ fJ}\).

\subsection{Oversampling}
Timing recovery and fractionally-spaced equalisation require the sampling rate \(f\) to be higher than the symbol rate \(R_s\). For DSP blocks that operate on the oversampled signal, the number of multiplications and additions can first be calculated per sample then scaled by the oversampling rate \(\frac{f}{R_s}\).

\subsection{Total Energy and Power}
The energy per symbol for the entire receiver is given by
\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{rx},s} = 2\sum_{k=1}^{L} N_A(k)E_A(n_k) + N_M(k)E_M(n_k),
\end{equation}
where the receiver is decomposed into \(L\) blocks that use different word lengths \(n_k\) according to their required precision and the factor of 2 is for the energy over both polarisations. The power is then
\begin{equation}
    P_\mathrm{rx} = R_sE_s.
\end{equation}
Converting to bits allows fair comparison across modulation schemes,
\begin{equation}
    E_\mathrm{rx} = \frac{E_{\mathrm{rx},s}}{\log_2(M)},
    \label{eq:rx_energy}
\end{equation}
where \(M\) is the modulation order.

\section{Transmitter Energy}
\subsection{Minimum Required SNR}
It is not sufficient to only compare the energy consumption of receiver implementations if they differ in performance. The CPON standard specifies a maximum pre-FEC bit-error rate (BER) of \(10^{-2}\) that must not be exceeded for the forward error-correction (FEC) scheme to function correctly. Each receiver implementation will therefore have a minimum required SNR to meet this FEC threshold. This SNR, together with the noise characteristics of the optical network, determines the minimum power at the transmitter.

\subsection{Noise}
\subsubsection{Shot Noise}
The relationship between SNR and transmitter power \(P_\mathrm{tx}\) depends on the dominant source of noise in the network. Optical access networks typically operate over short lengths and at low power. In this case amplifiers may not be needed and non-linear noise can be neglected. The dominant noise source is then shot noise. For the downstream PON link of \cref{fig:pon}, the 1:\(K\) splitter divides the transmitter power equally between the \(K\) ONUs, so each ONU receives a \(1/K\) share and sees an SNR of
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SNR} = \frac{P_\mathrm{tx} \cdot 10^{\frac{-\alpha L}{10}}}{2Kh\nu B},
\end{equation}
where \(B\) is the signal bandwidth, \(\alpha\) is the fibre loss in dB/km, and \(L\) is the fibre length. The factor of \(K\) in the denominator captures the splitter loss, so \(P_\mathrm{tx}\) is the OLT transmit power rather than the per-branch power. For receiver implementations with different FEC SNRs, the corresponding difference in transmitter power is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta P_\mathrm{tx} = P_\mathrm{tx,1} - P_\mathrm{tx,2} = 2Kh\nu B \cdot 10^\frac{\alpha L}{10}(\mathrm{SNR}_1 - \mathrm{SNR}_2)
\end{equation}
and the difference in energy per bit is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta E_\mathrm{tx} = \frac{1}{R_s}\left(\frac{P_\mathrm{tx,1}}{\log_2(M_1)} - \frac{P_\mathrm{tx,2}}{\log_2(M_2)}\right).
    \label{eq:tx_energy}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Amplifier and Non-Linear Noise}
In some cases the access network will operate over longer lengths and require EDFAs. Here the effects of amplifier and non-linear noise cannot be neglected.

For a single span, each EDFA with gain \(G\) and noise figure \(\mathrm{NF}\) (dB) contributes an ASE noise power
\begin{equation}
    P_\mathrm{ASE} = N_\mathrm{ASE}B = 10^{NF/10}h\nu (G-1)B.
\end{equation}
The Gaussian Noise (GN) model \cite{poggiolini2012gn} gives the single-span NLI power as
\begin{equation}
    P_\mathrm{NL} = \frac{C_\mathrm{NLI} P_\mathrm{tx}^3}{B^2},
\end{equation}
where \(C_\mathrm{NLI}\) depends on the fibre and span parameters.

For a link of \(n\) identical spans, both the ASE and NLI contributions from each span accumulate at the receiver, giving a total noise power of \(n(P_\mathrm{ASE} + P_\mathrm{NL})\). Including the 1:\(K\) splitter loss between the OLT and each ONU as in the shot-noise case, the per-ONU SNR is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SNR} = \frac{P_\mathrm{tx}}{Kn\left(N_\mathrm{ASE}B + \frac{C_\mathrm{NLI} P_\mathrm{tx}^3}{B^2}\right)},
    \label{eq:snr_nl}
\end{equation}
where, as before, \(P_\mathrm{tx}\) is the OLT transmit power and the splitter factor \(K\) ensures the derived power accounts for delivering the required SNR to every ONU. The required transmit powers can be solved for numerically from \cref{eq:snr_nl}, with the difference in transmitter energy following from \cref{eq:tx_energy}.

\subsection{Laser Efficiency}
The energy difference in \cref{eq:tx_energy} is the optical output energy of the transmitter laser. The true difference in energy consumed by the transmitter must account for the wall-plug efficiency \(\eta\) of the laser, giving
\begin{equation}
    \Delta E^{'}_\mathrm{tx} = \frac{1}{\eta} \Delta E_\mathrm{tx}.
    \label{eq:tx_energy_true}
\end{equation}

The total energy consumed to deliver one bit to every ONU is the sum of the transmitter energy (adjusted for laser efficiency) and the receiver energy across all \(K\) ONUs,
\begin{equation}
    E_\mathrm{sys} = \frac{1}{\eta}E_\mathrm{tx} + K E_\mathrm{rx}.
\end{equation}
When comparing two receiver implementations, the change in system energy per bit is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta E_\mathrm{sys} = \frac{1}{\eta}\Delta E_\mathrm{tx} + K\,\Delta E_\mathrm{rx},
    \label{eq:sys_energy}
\end{equation}
where \(\Delta E_\mathrm{tx}\) is given by \cref{eq:tx_energy} and depends on the noise model, and \(\Delta E_\mathrm{rx}\) is the change in receiver energy per ONU from \cref{eq:rx_energy}.
